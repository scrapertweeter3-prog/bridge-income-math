Bridge income is a withdrawal you never made

Pricing the ten years between a layoff and the pension

A layoff at 55 with a pension at 65 leaves a ten-year hole. The usual plan is to draw harder from the portfolio and let the pension refill the pot later. The plan that prices better is a job that covers part of the spending, because every dollar earned during the bridge is a dollar that stays invested with a decade of compounding still ahead of it.

Set the board. $1.2 million saved, $60,000 a year of spending, a 5% real return, pension checks starting at 65. Drawing the full $60,000 from the portfolio is a 5% withdrawal rate, and the balance round-trips: ten years later the pot is back to $1.2 million exactly. The bridge alone does not sink a plan sized like this; it never gets ahead either.

Run the bridge at three income levels:

Bridge incomePortfolio drawBalance at 65
$0$60,000$1,200,000
$12,000 (part-time)$48,000$1,350,935
$24,000 (consulting)$36,000$1,501,869

The income column is the lever. Cut the draw to $48,000 with $12,000 of part-time work and the pot ends at $1.35 million. Double the work to a $24,000 consulting practice and it ends at $1.50 million. Each dollar a year of bridge income is worth about $12.58 at 65, which is what a dollar withdrawn every year for ten years would have compounded to. A dollar the bridge earns is a dollar that never left the portfolio, and it compounds on the way through.

The assumption that breaks first is the income schedule. Bridge work is lumpy: the plan says $24,000, year one lands $9,000, and the draw rises to $51,000 while the spreadsheet still says $36,000. Log earned income like a ledger and re-run the table whenever a quarter comes in light; the pension date is fixed, the bridge is not.

This is the arithmetic behind the guides on FireNomics, where bridge income after a late-career layoff gets treated as a withdrawal schedule instead of a windfall. A bridge you can ledger is a bridge you can cross.
